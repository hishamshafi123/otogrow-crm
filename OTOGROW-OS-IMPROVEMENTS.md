## Mandatory business, workflow and design review for every item

User instruction, 2026-10-25: think deeply about each item's effect on the company and its workflows, question whether it is the right change, and implement the best version for Otogrow. The user explicitly extended this to UI, UX, design structure and design ideas: design quality is a first-class requirement for every item, not a cosmetic pass after functionality. This rule applies to every item and subtask, including delegated work. Completing a checklist entry is not sufficient evidence that the business problem has been solved or that the experience is clear and usable.

Before implementation, perform a proportionate but substantive review and record the decision and its supporting evidence:

1. **Define the business outcome.** Identify who needs the change, the actual problem, and the expected effect on delivery speed, creative quality, client experience, founder/Amna workload, capacity or margins. Choose an observable success check; distinguish expected benefits from measured results.
2. **Trace the complete workflow.** Map the current and proposed flow, including upstream inputs, responsible people/agents, handoffs, approvals, client interaction, exceptions and downstream consequences. Check whether the change removes a bottleneck or merely transfers work to someone else. Consider the affected service types and roles, not only the founder's screen or the happy path.
3. **Challenge the proposed solution.** Inspect the current implementation and requirements, test assumptions, and compare credible alternatives such as reusing existing capabilities, changing the process or building a new feature. The listed solution is a hypothesis to evaluate. If it is unnecessary, harmful or superseded, record the evidence and propose a better resolution; never silently drop the requirement or mark an unimplemented item complete.
4. **Assess the whole system.** Examine effects on related modules, data ownership, permissions and multiple-role access, human accountability, integrations, costs, reporting and existing records. Preserve explicit business rules, including independent final approval, scoped client communications and spending controls. Plan migration, failure recovery and rollback where relevant.
5. **Design the best complete experience.** Evaluate information architecture, navigation, page structure, terminology, visual hierarchy, typography, spacing, density, component patterns and interaction design together. Compare credible design ideas and select the clearest approach for Otogrow's non-technical staff and clients, explaining why it fits their tasks. Make the current state, next action, owner and result understandable; reduce unnecessary decisions, clicks and repeated entry. Design loading, empty, error, success, permission and recovery states as part of the flow. Check responsive layouts, keyboard/focus behavior, contrast and reduced motion. Maintain a coherent design system across the OS; reuse good patterns and improve poor ones deliberately. Backend-only changes must still assess their effects on latency, feedback, permissions and the user journey. More cards, animation or visual polish alone do not establish better UX.
6. **Choose the best practical implementation.** Optimize for a clear, complete user journey, reliable behavior, maintainability and realistic growth within Otogrow's constraints. Explain meaningful tradeoffs. Reuse sound infrastructure, avoid unnecessary complexity, and connect the UI, backend, permissions and operational behavior. Cosmetic fixes, placeholders and partially connected features do not satisfy a larger workflow requirement.
7. **Verify the outcome and consequences.** Define business, workflow and UX acceptance checks before coding, then validate the affected roles and workflows with appropriate tests and hands-on inspection. For UI changes, inspect the actual screens at relevant desktop and narrow sizes and walk through the interactions; passing code tests alone is insufficient. Include relevant errors, revisions, revoked access and recovery paths. Check adjacent workflows for regressions and whether the intended business and usability improvements are supported by evidence. Record anything that still needs real staff or production acceptance, and refine the design when inspection reveals friction or inconsistency.

Before marking an item complete, add a concise decision record under its ID: **problem and business outcome; current → proposed workflow; alternatives and chosen approach; UI/UX and design rationale; cross-system effects and tradeoffs; functional and design acceptance evidence; remaining limits.** Record conclusions and evidence, not private deliberation. Related items may share a review, but each must identify its own impact and acceptance evidence. A material implementation or design change requires revisiting the decision. Completion requires both working behavior and an inspected, coherent experience for the people using it.
